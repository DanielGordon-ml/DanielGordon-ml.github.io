LLM Agents MAS vs SAS 2025 Research

Single Agents Strike Back:
5 Counter-Intuitive Findings That Are Reshaping MAS in the LLM Era

G

Daniel Gordon • Multi-Agent Insights

April 21, 2026 • 12 min read

In classical MARL one agent that sees everything wins. Three new papers show the same is true for LLM agents — once you control for context and compute.

In classical multi-agent reinforcement learning, the wisdom is unambiguous: a single centralized agent with full observability crushes any team of specialists operating on partial views. Yet when LLMs entered the agentic stage, many of us assumed the game had changed. Massive context windows would be the new bottleneck—so decomposition into role-specialized multi-agent systems (MAS) became the default.

Three recent papers flip that assumption on its head. They show that, even (or especially) when we control for the very factors we thought favored MAS, single-agent systems (SAS) are shockingly competitive — often superior — and that many touted MAS gains were illusory.

01 When you equalize thinking-token budgets, single agents consistently match or outperform multi-agent architectures on multi-hop reasoning

The most information-theoretically clean result comes from Tran and Kiela (2025). They fix the global thinking token budget and compare SAS against five representative MAS designs across FRAMES and MuSiQue 4-hop datasets.

Result? SAS is the strongest or tied-for-strongest in the overwhelming majority of settings. Many prior “MAS wins” simply evaporated once extra compute and context were normalized.

“Many reported advantages of multi-agent systems are better explained by unaccounted computation and context effects rather than inherent architectural superiority.”

This is the direct counterpoint to the classic MARL intuition: coordination overhead and inter-message degradation act like partial observability penalties.

02 As frontier LLMs improve in long-context reasoning and tool use, the performance gap between MAS and SAS shrinks dramatically—sometimes reversing

Gao et al. (2025) ran the largest head-to-head study across 15 agentic tasks. Their headline observation:

As LLMs grow more capable, the performance gap narrows on complex tasks while MAS continues to face significant efficiency challenges. In simple tasks, MAS may even underperform SAS due to overthinking.

03 MAS failure rates remain stubbornly high (41–86 %) and the dominant causes are systemic design flaws, not model hallucinations

Cemri et al. (2025) open-sourced MAST-Data—1,642 annotated execution traces—and the first rigorous failure taxonomy (MAST).

These failures persist even when swapping stronger base models. The paper’s intervention studies show modest architectural tweaks can yield +9–15 % success-rate lifts.

04 MAS execution graphs contain identifiable node-, edge-, and path-level defects that pure scaling cannot fix

Gao et al. abstract MAS runs as a directed acyclic graph and isolate three recurring bottlenecks: node-level, edge-level, and path-level defects.

Their confidence-guided tracing method identifies the reward bottleneck agent, enabling targeted model upgrades that recover most accuracy gains at far lower cost.

05 Strategic hybrids (routing + cascade) deliver the best accuracy–efficiency Pareto frontier

Both Gao et al. and the broader literature converge: why not both? Agent-routing and agent-cascade paradigms yield 1.1–12 % accuracy gains while cutting costs by up to 88 %.

The road ahead

These papers collectively suggest that the context-window justification for pure MAS was real — but temporary and narrower than we assumed. As long-context reasoning, KV-cache efficiency, and test-time compute continue to improve, the old MARL wisdom that “one agent that knows all is much better” is reasserting itself faster than many MAS frameworks can adapt.

The truly interesting question for the next wave of research is no longer “MAS or SAS?” but “when and how should we deliberately introduce controlled partial observability and coordination overhead?”
